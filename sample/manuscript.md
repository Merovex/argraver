# The Departure

Jake Marion watched the barber pole, willing it to signal the airlock was open. His crew bickered and fought more than he had seen before. Jake wanted them off his ship. He wanted off his ship. They needed some contact with reality to recover from the emotional exhaustion of the two-pace hop to and from Rymok. Maybe they would get a furlough planetside. Green grass between their toes.

The barber pole flipped with a loud clunk—the signal to open the door.

Denny Reyes, his stocky engineer, turned the wheel, unlocking the hatch. As he pulled, the door opened on well-greased hinges. The station's air mixed with the stale air of the *Hard Bound*. Jake took a deep breath. After weeks of the ship's own breath—oil, ozone, the close warmth of too many men in too little hull—the dock air smelled of nothing, and nothing tasted fine.

He looked at Denny and Vance, his specialist, and the rest of the eight-man crew. The fresh air meant survival. Each of them carried that relief differently. Only Vance showed nothing. "Men, seventy-two hours. Then I want you all back here getting the ship ready." He traded glances with all of them. "No time to go to the surface."

Their shoulders slumped. Kuko was one of the crown jewels of the original four systems of the Knot.

"Not until the ship is ready for its next trip."

"Why don't we just get started then?" Vance asked.

Jake shook his head. "That last hop was rough. You might not feel it at your age, but we're all fatigued. Better to come back after a few days' rest so we're clearheaded as we make repairs."

Denny clapped Vance on his broad shoulder. "Come on. Time for some fun."

His crew filed into the station. The station's vents pulled the stale air of the *Hard Bound* past him as he returned to the ship proper. He would make one more pass through the ship before locking it up. It would give his men time to disappear into the depths of the station's mezzanine. He was not much older than they were, but they were not in command.

The pass through her was a captain's ritual and a mechanic's habit both. Jake went from aft to forward with one hand trailing the bulkheads, reading the ship the way he read a part. The reactionless drive still ticked as it cooled. The hull pinged and settled, shedding the strain of two crossings of the Soup and the hard acceleration to the station. She had brought them home. That was all he asked of her, and the one promise he made back: that he would put her right before he asked it again.

A quarter-hour later, he locked the mesh gate and stepped through the airlock to the station beyond. He pulled on his scally cap. Kuko Prime was their base of operations. As much as the *Hard Bound* felt like his home, his quarters were on the station.

Through the umbilical, he walked toward the station's hub. Jake paused when the transparent aluminum window gave him a panoramic view of Kuko Prime below. The deep blue of its oceans, white strokes of clouds, and muddled green and khaki of the land were breathtaking. He could see it from the *Hard Bound*, but the sheer breadth of the planet from the larger window was greater still. Jake rested a hand on the pane. The chill of it reached his palm. Down there were cities he would never set foot in and ports he would route around, a whole blue world he would only ever know through glass.

Jake entered his quarters. The automatic lights flickered on, casting a warm glow throughout. The pale wood paneling and well-watered plants calmed him by the time the door closed. He tossed his scally cap onto an open chair. The quarters held a stillness the ship never managed. No drives, no scrubbers, no watch changing at all hours—only the tick of the vents and the green smell of the plants his housekeeper kept alive while he was away.

Opening the refrigerator, he smiled. His housekeeper had cooked a casserole, and it looked spicy. After reheating it, he sat down at the table. Jake ate while reading through the mail on his slate. Reaching the end of his personal mail, he stopped. Nothing from home. He chuckled at the thought. Fifteen years since he left home and joined the Scouts. He made a point of heading home after every tour. Each time, it felt less like home. Nowhere *felt* like home. When his third tour ended, he chose not to visit. Now the mail from family stopped.

Jake put the glass container in the sink to soak before shifting to the official mail. The connection with Brixirjan was now fully established and relations were formalized. The Knot was officially six systems, though held together by one two-pace hop.

He pulled up the Scout Service classified report. The report speculated the four original systems of the Knot had at least five other connections with the three opened up with the Brixirjan Hop. None of those routes had yet opened, but three more opened with Belfasto and Rymok.

Jake remembered being a crewman on that hop. Centuries after the Soup collapsed and any hop more than one pace was guaranteed to fail, the Service's hop computer suggested two paces might be feasible again.

But since those suggestions came up every few decades with crews never returning, odds were not in their favor. A new hop drive was the only changed variable. That success formalized the Scout Service's charter. As the ship's engineer, Jake also won a promotion to ship captain and assignment to the *Hard Bound*.

On the last message, Jake paused. The timestamp showed it was sent soon after they emerged in Kuko. They wanted a new route traced from Rymok and he was to report to the Scout Service. *Two hops*. He set the slate down without a word.

* * *

The next morning, Jake walked to the Service office rather than take a lift. A long walk on a ship of 200 tons was not possible. The exercise gave him time to prepare for the meeting. The hub's main concourse was filling at that hour: dock crews coming off shift, a brace of merchant officers in pressed grays, a vendor cranking up the shutter of a noodle stall. Jake kept to the slow lane and let the traffic move around him. None of them knew where he was bound, or what waited at the end of the walk.

When he entered and took his cap off in the Service office, the receptionist motioned for Jake to head into Casmir Royce's office. Jake nodded and went in.

Royce looked up from his slate as Jake closed the door. "Captain Marion, how was the trip?"

Jake pointed at Casmir's slate with his cap. "It's in my report."

Casmir nodded at the slate. "Yes. I've read it. But how was it?"

Jake said nothing.

"Yes. Well, I suppose it is in the report." He motioned to the sitting area to Jake's left. "Fine. Please have a sit."

Jake sat on one of the two leather couches flanking an oval table. Casmir moved to the chair at the table's head and set the slate down.

The office did not belong on a frontier station. Real wood, and a great deal of it. A carpet that drank the sound out of the room. Behind the desk hung a framed star chart of the Knot, the four old systems inked in gold and the new routes drawn in a careful hand. Every piece of it had been hauled up a gravity well at ruinous cost and set just so, to say as much. Jake had grown up in a yard town where wood meant a crate.

"Tea?" Casmir pressed a buzzer.

Jake shook his head. He had eaten breakfast, with at least two cups of tea. Even then, he might accept a cup out of politeness. But Casmir reminded him of a salesman more than a businessman. A necessary evil to speak with. Jake took Casmir's offer for a ploy to make him more compliant.

A servant entered and poured a cup of tea. The man set the cup at Casmir's elbow, bowed a fraction, and withdrew without a word, as if he had been built for that and nothing else. [Jake had spent his last watch aboard flat on his back under a fouled scrubber.] The two facts shared the room and would not look at one another.

"Always down to business with you. You read your orders then."

"Two hops both at two paces. One is the new route to Rymok we opened last year. The other is to a system named Euakov. Russian?"

"It might have been. Records for that time period are a bit sketchy."

"So's the route to Rymok. Isn't Euakov supposed to be part of the Brixirjan side of the Knot?"

"Brixirjan's denying they have a route. We have our doubts. But you're right. The old maps show Euakov as just a hop away from Brixirjan."

It was one thing to risk a two-pace hop, but another altogether to face a second hop afterward. They would have to make the hop from Rymok to Euakov with the data provided by the Scout Service's planetary hop computer here on Kuko. There was not enough compute capacity for the AI of any ship to run the calculation on its own. It was an added layer of complexity when routes were already a delicate mathematical calculation. Then to get home they would need to reverse the route—twice.

"They're allies. Aren't we supposed to take them at their word? Why risk my crew?"

"Captain Marion—Jake, we don't know if they are holding back or not. You know about our competition with the Royal Scout Company, so you know we are in a race to open up every system we can before they do."

"Then why not attempt it from Brixirjan?"

"We don't have enough data from there to attempt it."

"But we do from Rymok? We've only had that route for a year. Who else besides me has made that hop?"

Casmir considered him over the rim of his cup, in no hurry, while the room's silence was broken only by the distant vibration of the station's machinery. His off hand had found his jacket pocket and stayed there.

Jake twirled the brim of his cap with both hands, knowing he was not being asked. "Director, I know I don't have a choice. It's an order."

"You can choose to resign your commission."

Jake stopped twirling his cap. "That's not an option."

Casmir smiled. "Then what do you need?"

"Your orders say 'soonest.' What does that mean?"

"Soonest."

Jake weighed it. "Captain's discretion, but don't dawdle. We have some ship components that are out of maintenance."

"Life threatening?"

"Aren't they all?"

"We can swap some of your crew so you can conduct repairs while in the Aether. Beef up the mechanical staff. We're not looking for a science patrol. Just prove we can make the hop so we have the route. Science can follow after."

"Why the rush? The Company?"

Casmir cocked his head in response.

"And my crew faces death over a charter."

"How is that different from your other missions? Jake, you know I was a scout once myself. I'm no stranger to danger." He offered it like a thing they shared, and waited to be agreed with.

"Yes, sir." Everyone knew Director Royce served as a scout. One tour, before the two-pace hops were successful. But he was a mate's apprentice straight out of training. As soon as he earned his rate, he found a permanent shore assignment. Ever since, he remained ashore. The only risk Royce faced in the decade or more since was the near-impossible destruction of this station in geosynchronous orbit over Kuko Prime.

Instead, he climbed the greasy pole as the Scout Service formed. He ordered Jake's former captain to make the first two-pace hop that gave the Scouts its charter. The pressure broke the captain's spirit. Jake took command and ensured the route was opened. Royce, who risked nothing, became director of the freshly chartered Scout Service.

"Take what you need to get ready. But as you say, don't dawdle."

"My men have two days left on their pass. I need the downtime, too."

"Fine. Then call it a fortnight to get underway?" Casmir picked up his slate, his preferred way to end conversations.

Jake stood and saluted. Casmir ignored him, so Jake turned and left.

# Chapter 2

The *Hard Bound* was splayed open as Jake toured from bow to stern. Each crew member remained focused on their repairs. None of them noticed him as he inspected their work—or if they did, none acknowledged him. Vance stocked the galley with foodstuffs: proto-protein and pastes, fiber, sugars. Components that, when mixed, would approximate meat, fruit, grains, albeit as casseroles. Vance said it was marginally better tasting than prison food. They were more compact than the real thing—or even synthetic foods.

The ship lay gutted for repairs. Conduit hung from unshipped panels, and the air carried the bite of sealant and scorched insulation, sharp enough to coat the back of the throat. Somewhere aft a grinder shrieked against a frame member, died, and shrieked again. Mikel had the deck plates up in the companionway; Jake stepped the gaps from long habit, one hand to the cold bulkhead. Two hundred tons left a man little room to pass another. On the *Hard Bound* you learned the width of your own shoulders, or you wore the bruises.

Back in his stateroom, he sat, momentarily satisfied with their work.

The stateroom held a bunk, a fold-down desk, and the ship's stubborn smell of machine oil, warm electronics, and the trace of a man who slept where he worked. The deck thrummed under his stocking feet, faint and constant—the station's power feeding the *Hard Bound* through the umbilical. He set the back of his hand to the bulkhead. The cold came through at once, the cold that never left a hull this small—a plate and a prayer between a man and the Dark. You made your peace with it, or you stayed dirtside and let other men do the crossing.

All that remained was a trip outside to view the dings in the hull they picked up when they approached the starport at Kuko Prime. Denny spent half his time out on the hull working on them. That gave Jake confidence the work was done right. But without the gravatic radiation barrier they would not survive a single hop. Jake took his boots off to change into his EV suit's inner skin.

"Captain requested at the bridge."

Jake huffed as he mashed the intercom button. "What is it?"

"You have a visitor." The crewman's voice was young. Reni Otero. A wiper.

"Who?"

After a lengthy pause. "He refuses to answer." Reni's voice wavered.

Jake raised an eyebrow and pulled his boots back on. Few could come onto a ship without the captain's leave. Fewer could strike fear in a young recruit. As he walked the short way to the bridge, he braced himself for an unscheduled meeting with the Director. It could only be bad news.

Three days from departure, and the Director had come aboard in person. That alone set Jake on edge. Royce did not come to a man; he summoned the man to him, to that wood-paneled office with its gold star chart and its tea no one wanted. An order from Royce arrived on the slate—a line of text a captain could read twice and obey, or stare at across a shift before answering. For the Director to leave his office, cross the dock, and come up the umbilical of a two-hundred-ton scout meant the decision was already made, and he had come to watch Jake swallow it. The old tightness settled in Jake's chest, the one he wore through every inspection and every audit. Whatever waited on the bridge, it was not a favor.

He entered the bridge. Director Royce sat in the captain's chair—the only seat on the bridge. The walk in from the station had left him a touch short of breath, though he wore it like a man who had merely chosen to sit. Jake took a deep breath and exhaled. He rarely sat in the chair. It was bad luck. Not that Jake believed in luck. But it was a tradition from the earliest days of man's climb into the stars that Jake respected.

Older even than that. The custom came down from Old Sol, from a coastal service whose name had worn to almost nothing in the carrying. No one Jake knew could say what the coast was, or what such men had guarded across nine hundred years and a dark age between. Captains kept a chair on the bridge that no one sat, and the reason had not survived the Silence. Some said it honored the ship. Some said the dead. Jake did not know which, and the men who could have told him were long dust. He knew only that every captain he ever served under left the seat empty, and so did he.

"What do we owe the pleasure, Director?"

"Nice of you to join me, Jake. I'm surprised to see you off the bridge."

Jake squinted. "Sir, we're still in dock. Besides, you can't run a ship from the bridge alone."

Royce swiveled the chair toward Kuko Prime's curved horizon. The planet shaded the station from its yellow dwarf primary star. The billions on the continent below were asleep, or soon would be.

Beyond the canopy the starport sprawled in tiers of worklight, gantries and fuel booms throwing long shadows across the berthed hulls. A tug crawled between them, its strobe pulsing red against the white. Past the rim of the station the planet filled the black, a dark crescent rimmed in the amber of its dwarf sun, the nightside scattered with the dim grid of cities. Cold light, all of it. Out here the warmth ran out fast, and a man learned to take what he could get from a yellow star ninety million kilometers gone.

"I would have thought you more delegatory in your command."

"*Hard Bound* is only two hundred tons. I have eight souls under my command. That's not enough to delegate either leadership or command."

"I thought the standard was to add a first officer when you have more than five. It was in my day."

Jake held his tongue. Royce served aboard a ship ten times *Hard Bound*'s size, and three times its crew. "We're a small ship, sir."

Royce tutted. "Nevertheless, I would expect the burdens of command are hard even with a crew of eight?"

Jake worried about where the conversation was heading. The last thing he wanted was a corpo bothering him or his crew. He rested an elbow on the astrogation console. "I've never had a problem with command before. Where is this going?"

Royce pushed himself out of the chair. He paced before wheeling and facing Jake. "How are the ship's preparations?"

"Good."

Royce nodded. "How about the hop data from the planetary AI?"

Jake answered with a raised eyebrow.

"Yes. I see. You're not a man who leaves things to chance."

"Well, that's why you have me handle all the tough hops. Isn't it?"

"Yes, quite right."

"You're stalling. Why don't you tell me why you're on my ship, sitting in my chair?"

Royce cleared his throat. "It has been decided that you will take along a first officer, of sorts."

"A first officer?"

"Yes. The Board made that determination."

"Why do I need a first officer?"

Royce smiled, unbothered. "We don't question your skills, if that's what you mean."

Jake stared hard, silent.

"In fact, that's the reason why we are assigning you a first officer."

"Assigning?"

"Yes. The Board made that determination."

"So the Board decided it would be in my best interest to have a first officer underfoot while we attempt a reconnection with a system we first reached only a year ago. Without consulting me."

"The Board needn't consult with you on personnel issues."

"This is deeply personal, Casmir. A captain picks his crew."

"Well, not this time."

Jake studied him. "All right, who is it?"

"Tarquin Ashby—"

"Never heard of him."

"—Bennet."

The name slapped Jake. The Bennets were one of the more powerful families in the Old Knot. Not a ruling family, but their voice was heard by those who did rule. The Bennets were the sort of family that created the Royal Scouts as rival to the the independent merchants who backed the Scout Service. "Why?"

Jake knew the shape of a family like that without ever having stood in one of their halls. The Bennets did not crew ships; they owned the yards that built them and held the routes the ships ran. A man born to that name never wondered whether he would eat, or whether the next hop would scatter him across half a light-year of Soup. He wondered only how high he would climb. Jake had clawed up from a yard town with grease worked into his knuckles and a debt to the Service for every rung. The distance between those two starting lines could not be crossed in one lifetime. It did not need to be. The Bennets simply bought the lifetimes of the men who could.

"Not an answer I'm sure I can give with any confidence."

Jake shook his head. "No. Not even as a stowaway. Has he even been to the Soup? The Dark?"

"He has as much experience as I do."

Jake did not care that Royce meant for the statement to stop him questioning the Bennet's experience. "None." He pointed at Reni. "He's as useful as a wiper. He will only get in the way."

Royce held up his hands to stop Jake. The color rose in his face, and his jaw set hard. He had climbed through a Service that measured a man by what he could do, not the name he carried—and Jake had set him level with a wiper. "Calm down. You can't speak like that." Then the flush receded, and the affable mask settled back over it.

"I am captain of this boat." He tipped his head toward the window. "Planetside, he may hold sway. But out in the Dark—in the Soup—a captain is a god. He won't follow orders. And I don't want him giving any." He said it without heat.

"He won't."

Jake faced him. Despite his station, he held Royce's eye and did not look away. "He had better not. A ship cannot serve two masters. It will be dangerous out there."

Royce chuckled. "How difficult can it be? Once you hop there's nothing to do but wait to emerge."

Wait to emerge. As though the Soup were a road and not a drowning. Jake had hung in that gray nothing with the hull groaning around him and the instruments telling him lies, had counted the hours by his own pulse while the pollution pressed at the seams and a green crew prayed. Men went into the Soup whole and came out wrong, or did not come out at all. Royce had read about it in a report. *He thinks the danger is a number on a page.* There was the whole trouble, sitting in Jake's chair.

"You've never left Kuko. Have you?"

Royce squared his shoulders. "I'll make this easy for you. Jake, either you accept Tarquin or you're out of the Service. Do you understand? The Board unanimously approved his assignment as first officer of the *Hard Bound*."

There was the same threat as before. They knew Jake found purpose in the Scouts. It was a leash they yanked when it pleased them. "Would they put him in command of the *Hard Bound* if I refuse?" Jake knew the question was absurd. He hoped Royce would handle the subtext that a first officer should be captain worthy in his own right.

"He won't be the first officer. Just in name only."

"Those types don't know the difference. You know that." Royce was not from a family. He grew up better than Jake, but not by much. That had to matter here.

"He has to have the title. Otherwise, he's a tourist. You complain that members of the Board don't understand what you do. He'll end up on the Board. Think of it as your chance to shape the future of the Board."

Jake went still. The pieces slid together—the title, the Board seat Royce dangled, the careful way he laid it all out. *This was never about the ship.* "There's more to it, isn't it? We write history with each system creation. A Bennet gets his name added to the record."

Royce shifted his weight and would not meet Jake's eye. "Here's the bad news."

"Bennet wasn't the bad news?"

"He's to get the credit for this connection."

Something cold settled in his gut. He would crack the hop, hold the ship through the Soup, open a dead route by his own hand—and the record would carry a Bennet who had never seen it. *Forty years on, the histories will not know my name.* Jake spit. "Well, that's the truth of it. The Bennets write themselves into history. And a Marion out." He turned to the work that was his, credit or no.

# Chapter 3

Centuries ago, an architect designed the vast openness of the mezzanine to ward off the claustrophobic feeling of being on a station for months at a time. But for someone accustomed to the tightness of a ship, it was a vacuum pulling the air out of his lungs. Jake pulled his scally cap down to his eyebrows to blind him to the domed wall climbing up.

Once he agreed to take a lubber on as first officer, Director Royce insisted they get acquainted. He agreed to meet Tarquin on the station rather than the *Hard Bound* to protect the crew. Jake arrived ten minutes early for the meeting. Jake sipped his beer then tapped on the restaurant's menu.

He watched as clusters of people came together to meet, and went into any of a dozen eateries and pubs. None noticed him. Not that he mattered on a station this massive. The station had to be home to at least ten thousand permanent and temporary workers and visitors. He checked his watch. Twenty minutes late. Jake thought about leaving. Rather than think of Tarquin as an aristocrat, he asked himself, *how much time would I give my old captain?* He might not give ten minutes to an aristocrat. But for the captain? Hours. 

This was not the first order he had been made to choke down in his career. But this one stuck in his throat more than any had in years. There had been fewer difficult orders as he ascended the ranks. Once he made captain, they had all but disappeared.

Throughout his career, the Scout Service proved itself to be a scrappy meritocracy. Work hard. Be competent. Wait your turn. Then take the opportunity when it presented itself. That's what helped Jake's career climb. One rung at a time.

What angered him was how Tarquin fit into this. Nothing spoke to his competence. He was a lubber who had a token understanding of what it meant to be a Scout—like Royce now that Jake thought of it. Neither man risked their lives or their crew's. Jake had more certified flight time on the loo than Royce had off planet or station.

He finished his beer and ordered another one. Tarquin was the sort who bought his way into a career. But what would he do with it? Would he dedicate himself to reconnecting humanity? Or would he line his pocket and add to the Bennet family's notoriety?

As the waiter brought his beer, a man in an immaculate Scout dress uniform approached. Jake took one look at the single row of ribbons. They were all participation ribbons. The one everybody gets for surviving Scout School. A second had a device affixed that spoke to two honorable tours of service. The third he did not recognize, but its being in third place confirmed it was less meaningful. On the young man's lapels were the pips of a commander. The soft, easygoing look told Jake he had his man. Jake stood up. 

"Tarquin Ashby de Bennet?"

Tarquin stopped mid-stride. "How did you know it was me?"

Jake shrugged as he motioned to a spare chair. "The cut of your uniform, I guess." (Need to relocate this next bit to where it makes sense) Jake's uniform was more relaxed—dark brown pants with a gold command stripe, a white shirt, and a low-profile armored vest frequently used by undercover security. What Tarquin could not see was the small pistol Jake carried inside his waistband or the ribbons he refused to wear.

Tarquin smoothed the front of his unwrinkled tunic and winced. "Too much is it?"

"Not at all. First impressions, right?" Jake signaled a waiter.

Tarquin sat and slid his chair forward. "Right."

The waiter handed Tarquin a menu. He flipped and looked at either side. "I don't know. Kukan Steak?" He looked at Jake. "Steak?"

Jake smiled. "Only if you're buying."

"Then two steaks and chips. And a pint. Something hearty. And another of whatever he's drinking."

The waiter nodded, retrieved the menus and headed to the kitchen.

"You know, Captain Marion, I've followed your career since I was a boy."

"Not likely. I only recently made captain. Engineers aren't the sort that the media follow."

"Then call me a unicorn. You've served aboard the *Hard Bound* for three tours now. She's made a name for herself. A crew as small as hers is as easy to follow as any musical ensemble. You might only have recently become its lead man. But from what I read, you've been its backbone far longer."

"Flattery like that won't earn you any scout points, Sir Bennet."

Tarquin waved his hand. "Honorifics won't be necessary. Jake is it?"

Jake's third beer arrived with Tarquin's. "Captain will be fine for now."

"Well, call me Tarquin."

"So tell me a little about yourself."

Tarquin sipped the film off his beer then quaffed. "Given this is an interview, I take it you've already seen my service record."

"Paper is no measure of a man, Tarquin. If it is, then he's got no business being in this business. He's the kind of man that will get other men killed."

Tarquin slapped the table and smiled. "That's what I expected, Jake. Blunt."

"The Deep brooks no delay."

Tarquin rocked as he nodded. "And I would expect that to continue while you're serving on my ship."

Jake canted his head. "Unless you have orders otherwise, *Hard Bound* is my ship."

Tarquin smiled and patted Jake's hand as it rested on the table. "You may be its captain, Jake. But I'll be the ranking member of the crew. Remember the honorific."

Jake did not care about the honorific. Rank in the Dark meant nothing. If Vance or Denny told him the ship would be lost if he gave the wrong order, he would not just pull rank on them. He would stop to understand why. But those were competent men. He read Tarquin's file. It was brief, nothing to offer a crew in need. A captain was a god. Would Tarquin understand?

The waiter brought the steaks, cutting the tension.

"We're getting off on the wrong foot. You asked me to tell you about myself." He put the napkin in his lap, cut a piece of steak and chewed while he continued. "I'm on my third tour. I flew in the dark on the first. Mother was worried, so father had me grounded. Mostly liaison and staff work since then. A bit of time working for Administration. I've been licensed to fly, but not certified on the Strand class." He took another bite. "Not enough hours."

Jake measured his words to keep his composure. "So you're neither technically proficient nor tactically competent to understand the feel of a ship."

With the steak knife in his right hand he pointed at Jake. "Yet."

"You've followed me, so you know I have several tours on a Strand class. The *Hard Bound*."

"This is your fourth. But it's what you love doing, right? Some men are born for the Dark. It's put some gray in your temples, though."

"It's not an easy life."

Tarquin set down his cutlery. Resting his elbows on the table, he leaned over to Jake. "You were born for this. I was born for something else. This needn't be adversarial. I won't impose on you. I wouldn't dare to understand what it is you do as captain. And I doubt an entourage as tight as yours would listen to me if I did. You have nothing to worry about there. But I'll still be the ranking member on the boat."

Jakes' face warmed up.

"Relax. This is good for both of us. I need more of an understanding of what scouts do. You'll need someone in your corner within the Administration, or on the Board."

That caught Jake off guard. When he spoke with Royce, he wondered why the Board assigned the young man to his ship. He doubted it was to replace him. But stranger things were bound to happen as he climbed the ranks. Now he realized this was all about helping an aristocrat. That was fine, as long as he left Jake and his men alone. "You on the Board?"

Tarquin returned to his steak, and shrugged. "Why not? With this mission under my belt, I'll have a greater understanding of what you do than any of the board members today."

Jake watched Tarquin, then looked at his steak. It was a massive slice of beef. This was not the proto-protein woven into a meat-like consistency with flavor added to give it a beefy taste and texture. He popped a chip into his mouth. The texture was nearly foreign to him after eating a synthesized alternative. And it was far more than he could afford on his salary. He pulled his utensils out and cut his first piece.

* * *

Jake walked through the Scout Service headquarters anteroom and past the receptionist. He opened the door into Royce's office.

Royce looked up from his slate as Jake approached. His receptionist hurried to catch up. He held up his hand to stop the receptionist.

"I take it things did not go well."

"No they did not."

Royce sighed and tossed his slate on the table. He motioned to the couch. Jake shook his head.

Rather than hide behind his desk, Royce came around and leaned against its edge next to Jake. "What happened?"

"The kid thinks he's going to run the ship."

"You do know he's a few years younger than you."

"By experience, he's a child. I respect that he's from an aristocratic family. That's as natural as breathing. But out there, I can't have him barking orders at my men. It's going to happen."

"You want me to talk to him?"

"Didn't you already?" Jake jabbed a finger at a wall. "Out there where the only thing between you and annihilation is a few millimeters of metal, I need him to know his place. Him thinking he's in charge won't cut it."

Royce sighed. "I thought he understood that when I explained it to him."

"It ought to be in writing. I know the Board wants him to take credit. Fine. And maybe that order will confuse things. Rescind it when you're back for all I care. Just don't let him kill my men. Maybe explain to him that killing my men means killing him, too."

Jake left with Royce's promises and assurances. He never knew whether to trust the man. He wanted to. After all, he was director of the Recontact Branch. Royce had never acted falsely with him. *But the man is a politician*. He had not become a Board member by always standing up for what was right. He got there by getting along. That meant compromises. Compromises Jake did not think he could ever make.

Would Royce convince the Board? Jake hoped so. But as captain, he knew hope was not something you relied upon. He would only rest easy when he saw the amended orders that clarified his authority. Not that he needed it. Tarquin would.

At least he didn't threaten to fire him this time.
